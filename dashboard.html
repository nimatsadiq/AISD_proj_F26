Why a short list instead of an alarm

Each month there are about 50 things you could worry about: rice in Borno, tomatoes in Yobe, onions in Adamawa, and so on. Of those 50, roughly 7 will actually jump in price. The other 43 will not. Nobody knows in advance which.

will actually jump (7) will stay steady (43)

We could set the system to catch nearly all of them. But to do that, it has to point at 29 of the 50. Handing someone 29 names out of 50 is the same as telling them to watch the whole market, which is no help at all.

flagged by an alarm (29 of 50)

It is the smoke alarm so sensitive it goes off when you make toast. It really does catch every fire. It also gets unplugged by the end of the week.

So instead of flagging, we rank. All 50 are sorted by how worried the model is, and only the top few are shown. Of 5 shown, roughly 2 turn out to be real. Some are missed. But 5 is a list a person can act on, and a food on that list is about two and a half times as likely to rise as one picked at random. That multiple is the Lift figure above, and it moves as you change the length of the list: a shorter list is sharper, a longer one catches more.

That is the trade: catch nearly everything and be ignored, or catch less and be used. Drag the slider above to watch it move.

What this cannot do. It does not say how large the rise will be, and it cannot see a policy shock coming, such as a fuel subsidy being removed or the naira being floated, because nothing in past prices announces those.
